Two ways to use TRACE

For student learning

Try first → Reveal reasoning → Annotate → Check → Evolve.

The learner attempts, explains, inspects, and revises. AI may question or reflect; it does not supply the finished thinking.

For a bounded AI job

Task → Relevant context → Actions and aids → Checks → Exit and escalation.

The teacher names what the agent may read, produce, and change—then defines how it will be checked and where human review resumes.

1. See TRACE in a real classroom task

Teacher-created practice question: Evaluate whether schools should use generative AI to support student feedback.

T — Target

Student thinking

Make a defensible judgment using a claim, relevant evidence, a counterclaim, and a reasoned conclusion.

R — Retrieve

Attempt before assistance

Write for five minutes from memory. Mark the sentence where your judgment becomes visible.

A — Ask with boundaries

AI role

Ask only diagnostic questions. Do not rewrite, score, add facts, or provide a model answer.

C — Check

Verification

Compare the response with teacher criteria and approved class sources. Treat AI comments as suggestions, not authority.

E — Edit and explain

Human handoff

Revise one paragraph, then finish: “I changed… because… I still need to check…”

Bounded prompt to try:
You are a questioning coach. Read this anonymous practice paragraph. Ask no more than three questions about the claim–evidence connection, counterclaim, and final judgment. Do not rewrite the paragraph, add facts, score it, or provide a model answer. Stop after the questions.

2. Build your bounded AI job

Type directly into this private browser worksheet, then print it. Nothing entered here is saved or sent.

  • No identifiable student information is included.
  • The learner makes a genuine attempt before assistance.
  • The AI cannot publish, submit, grade, or message anyone.
  • A human checks the result before it affects a student.

3. Student handout — TRACE your revision

Name: __________________________   Task: __________________________

Quick example — what am I supposed to do?

My first attempt: “AI feedback is always fair because computers are not biased.”
My bounded prompt: Ask me up to three questions about my evidence, counterclaim, and judgment. Do not rewrite my answer.
My reflection: “I changed ‘always fair’ because I had not supported it and AI systems can reflect bias. I still need to check an approved source.”
  • I attempted the task before asking AI.
  • I can explain the idea without reading the AI response.
  • I checked the response against class criteria or an approved source.
  • I identified one useful suggestion and one possible weakness.
  • I revised in my own words and can explain what I changed.
  • I did not share names, private records, or sensitive information.

4. Run it tomorrow: a 15-minute routine

Attempt → Inspect → Revise · 15 minutes

  1. Students answer one practice question from memory for five minutes.
  2. They use an approved tool or teacher-provided AI response to inspect—not replace—their attempt.
  3. They mark where evidence, explanation, and evaluation are visible.
  4. They revise one paragraph and add: “I changed… because…”
  5. The teacher reviews the reflection, not an AI-generated score.

Research boundary: Retrieval, informative feedback, prompted self-explanation, and structured self-assessment support the design rationale for an attempt–inspect–revise loop. They do not prove that TRACE or these applications improve IB results.

Why this is designed this way

The sources below support individual design ingredients—not guaranteed outcomes and not validation of TRACE.

  1. Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Test-Enhanced Learning — delayed retention after retrieval practice.
  2. Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021), Retrieval Practice Consistently Benefits Student Learning — systematic classroom review.
  3. Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), The Power of Feedback Revisited — feedback effects vary with information and context.
  4. Bisra et al. (2018), Inducing Self-Explanation — meta-analysis of prompted explanation.
  5. Bastani et al. (2025), Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning — one high-school mathematics field experiment.
